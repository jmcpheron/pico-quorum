A design study · Picoquorum Handheld

A signing console you hold in two hands.

A Pico 2 W, a 2.4″ screen, a knob and a Sign button under a flip cover, in a flat printed case you hold like a handheld game or lay on the desk. The key is a cartridge: it slides down into a bay in the back, and clicks onto its plug.

What's in it

Size
86 × 147 × 30 mm; 155 mm tall with a key in
Board
Raspberry Pi Pico 2 W, wires soldered to its pads
Screen
2.4″ IPS, 240 × 320, SPI
Controls
An EC11 knob to turn and push. A tactile Sign button under a flip cover
Key
key-c, an ATECC608C behind USB-C, in a 48 × 54 mm snap-shut cartridge
Case
Eight printed parts and the cartridge's two, M3 screws into heat-set inserts
Firmware
The console image as is: BOARD = "build1", SCREEN = "2.4"

A first draft. Designed and checked in CAD: 243 pairs of parts clear each other, and the cartridge clears everything as it slides in. Nothing is printed, wired or run yet, and the 2.4″ screen hasn't been seen working. Measure your parts first; the README says which.

Same console, same rules

  1. 1

    Turn to read

    The knob pages through what a transaction does, in plain language. Owner and threshold changes are red pages.

  2. 2

    Lift the cover

    Sign sits in a well under a flip cover, so it isn't pressed by accident.

  3. 3

    Hold to sign

    Only after every page is seen, and only while held: 2 s, or 3 s when a page is red. Let go early and nothing is signed.

  4. 4

    The key stays in its chip

    key-c signs the Safe transaction hash the console worked out itself. Its private key never leaves the chip.

The key is a cartridge

key-c, the USB-C board with the secure element on it, snaps into a 48 × 54 mm cartridge. The cartridge slides down into a bay in the back, the way a handheld game takes one: its label out of the back, its top standing 8 mm out of the top edge to pull it by.

  • Grooves guide it. A tongue down each side runs in a groove, so the USB-C plug at the bottom is never the guide.
  • One way in. A rib in one corner fits a notch in the cartridge. Upside down, it stops 2 mm before the plug.
  • A click. Two bumps in the bay's walls click into its sides when it's home.
  • Swap keys by swapping cartridges. Each has a frame on the back for a label.

Four colours from one plate

The front plate prints face down, so its face is its first layers. Change filament three times, at 0.6, 1.2 and 1.8 mm, and each depth of cut shows its own colour: the name in charcoal, TURN and SIGN in red, the wells in blue. No paint, no inlays.

The depths, colours and words are settings in handheld.py. Not checked on a real print yet.

Build it

Sixteen steps, from printed parts to a key in its bay. Each picture is drawn from the case's own model, so it follows the CAD's numbers: new printed parts in amber, what's done in white, and what the step needs along the bottom.

Print it

Every file is the way up it prints, flat on the bed, with no supports to start with. GitHub shows each in 3D; its download button saves the STL. Nothing has been printed yet, so try the new parts first, in this order:

  1. cartridge_tray.stl, cartridge_cover.stl: the key's cartridge, each half on its outside face. PETG if you have it: the clips are close to PLA's limit.
  2. handheld_bay_test.stl: the tub's top only: the bay, the plug's holder and two corner posts. Try the cartridge's fit here first.
  3. handheld_plug_lock.stl: holds the key's plug up in its holder.
  4. handheld_plate.stl: the front plate, face down; four colours by filament changes.
  5. handheld_body.stl: the tub, on its back. The bay's floor is a 48.6 mm bridge: supports there only if it sags.
  6. handheld_carrier.stl, handheld_spacers.stl: behind the screen, with the Pico's posts; the four spacers.
  7. handheld_guard_base.stl, handheld_guard_cover.stl: the Sign button's flip cover and its base.
  8. handheld_sign_cap.stl: the Sign button's cap, head down.

If the cartridge is tight in the bay, sand the bumps. The numbers to change are in handheld.py, and the guide's printing notes say which.

What you need

  • Bought: a Pico 2 W, the 2.4″ screen, an EC11 knob and cap, a 12 × 12 mm tactile switch and a mini breadboard, a USB-C plug on a short cable, two 4.7 kΩ resistors, a key-c.
  • Hardware: ten M3 heat-set inserts, ten M3 × 8 and six M2 × 8 socket heads, six M3 washers, four rubber feet, 20 mm of 1.75 mm filament for the hinge.
  • Tools: a soldering iron with an insert tip, 2.5 and 1.5 mm hex keys, flush cutters, a meter.

The tub

  1. 1. An insert in each corner post

    Let each one sink in under the iron's own weight. Don't twist. Stop when it's flush.

  2. 2. The feet

    Four rubber strips into their pockets, round ends into the round ends.

  3. 3. The key's USB-C plug

    Pull-ups soldered at the plug first. Then up through the foot of the key's bay, pointing up, and the lock clicks in under it.

  4. 4. The breadboard and the Sign switch

    Between its four stops. The switch goes on the 3rd column from the right: that's under the button.

The plate

  1. 5. An insert in each of the carrier's bosses

    Plate face down on a towel. Six: four round the window, two lower down for the carrier's tail.

  2. 6. The Sign button's guard

    The cover hinges on a pin of 1.75 mm filament. The cap goes up through the base from underneath.

  3. 7. The guard into its well

    Two M2 screws. Shut, the cover is flush with the face.

  4. 8. The knob

    In from behind, its nut on in the well. Snug, not tight.

  5. 9. The screen onto its pins

    Glass down, its 8-pin header on the left.

  6. 10. The spacers

    A printed spacer on each pin.

  7. 11. The carrier

    Six M3 screws with washers. Tighten the four round the screen evenly: they hold it down.

  8. 12. The Pico

    Solder its wires first, with the board in your hand. Then onto the posts on the carrier's tail, below the screen, USB to the right edge.

  9. 13. The wires

    Screen and knob to the Pico. Try it on the bench before it goes in the tub.

Close it, and the cartridge

  1. 14. Onto the tub

    The key's plug and the button's jumpers to the Pico. Then the plate on, four socket heads into the counterbores.

  2. 15. The key into its cartridge

    A peg through the key's lanyard hole, the cover on until four clips click. The chip shows through a window; a label goes on the back.

  3. 16. The knob's cap, then the key into its bay

    Turn it over. The cartridge goes in from the top edge, label toward you, down the grooves until it clicks. Done.

Then: make it an owner

Built, it's a Picoquorum console like any other. Run the bench check, set it up for your Safe, and add its key as one owner. The owner's guide walks through it, and the app checks every confirmation and executes.
